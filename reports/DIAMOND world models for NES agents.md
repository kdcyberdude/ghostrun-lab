# Borrow DIAMOND's ideas, not its dreams

No. Neither DIAMOND nor any other pre-trained world model will teach your agent what NES entities do, or carry one generic model through all 32 Super Mario Bros levels plus Contra, Adventure Island, Kage and Final Mission. Nothing you can download models the NES. DIAMOND ships 26 Atari models at 64×64 and one CS:GO model. The only Mario checkpoint with weights (PlayGen) models a Java re-implementation of Mario at 128×128. The one model family trained on 2D platformers (Genie) is closed. The worry behind the hope is also upside down. Samples are your cheapest resource: your emulator makes DIAMOND's whole 5.5M-frame CS:GO dataset in about seven minutes. The real price of a learned model is 1–12 GPU-days on the same RTX 5090 that PPO already keeps busy. Even a perfect model would miss your real bottleneck. World-model agents score near zero on hard-exploration games: DIAMOND scored 114 on Private Eye, where humans score 69,571, and MuZero scored 0 on Montezuma's Revenge after 20B frames. The reason is simple. A model trained on pipes where Down did nothing confidently predicts that Down does nothing on the one pipe that matters. A diffusion model is also slower than the game it copies. DIAMOND's published imagination rate is about 1,600 transitions/s on an RTX 4090, against your 13,900 real decisions/s. Keep the emulator as your world model. Take DIAMOND's lesson as a small event-prediction head inside PPO. Find exceptions like the pipe with (place, button) counts and branch probes.

## DIAMOND is a 4M-parameter dreamer built for 100k samples

DIAMOND (Alonso et al., NeurIPS 2024 spotlight) is a diffusion model that draws the next game screen. Its denoiser is a plain 2D U-Net of about **4M parameters** that predicts the next **64×64 RGB frame**. It sees the **last 4 frames**, stacked as image channels next to the noisy target. It also sees the **last 4 actions**, which enter through adaptive group normalisation in every residual block ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399)). It uses the EDM diffusion formulation instead of DDPM. EDM trains the network to predict the clean image at high noise, so rollouts stay stable with very few denoising steps. Over 1,000-step Breakout rollouts, DDPM with 10 steps drifted more than EDM with one (same source). DIAMOND samples with **3 Euler steps per frame**. A single step blurs futures that can branch, such as the Boxing opponent. Cutting to one step lowered the mean human-normalised score (HNS) on the top ten games from 3.05 to 1.96 (same source). A separate CNN-LSTM predicts the reward, as one of three classes (its sign only), and the end of an episode. A CNN-LSTM actor-critic plays the game. It is trained only on **15-step imagined rollouts**, using REINFORCE with a value baseline and λ-returns. The whole agent has 13M parameters (same source).

DIAMOND was built for the Atari 100k benchmark. That means **100k agent decisions per game** at frameskip 4, about two hours of play, collected by **one environment**. Training alternates four phases for 1,000 epochs: collect 100 real steps, then train the diffusion model, then the reward and termination model, then the actor-critic in imagination ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399); [trainer config](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond/blob/main/config/trainer.yaml)). One run takes **about 2.9 days on one RTX 4090** and about 12 GB of VRAM. The paper's 130 runs used 1.03 GPU-years. Most of that time goes on dreaming, not on fitting the model. Of the 543 ms per update, imagination for the actor-critic takes 340 ms and the diffusion model 88 ms (same source).

The CS:GO model was added after the paper was accepted. It scales the design to **381M parameters**: a 330M dynamics model at 56×30 plus a 51M diffusion upsampler to 280×150. It was trained without any RL on **87 hours (5M frames) of human Dust II play**. Training took **12 days on one RTX 4090**, and the model runs at about 10 Hz on an RTX 3090 ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399); [csgo branch](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond/tree/csgo)). Its configuration adds stability tricks that Atari did not need: noise on the context frames, 4-step autoregressive training, and offset noise ([CS:GO config](https://huggingface.co/eloialonso/diamond/blob/main/csgo/config/agent/csgo.yaml)).

On Atari 100k, DIAMOND reached a **mean HNS of 1.459** and an IQM of 0.641, and beat humans on 11 of 26 games. At publication that was the best mean for an agent trained purely in a world model, ahead of STORM (1.266), DreamerV3 (1.097) and IRIS (1.046) ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399)). The headline hides a skew. A few very high games carry the mean, and DIAMOND's **median of 0.373 is below STORM's 0.580** ([EDELINE, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00466v2)). The split across games is the fact that matters for your project:

The authors credit their wins to games "where capturing small details is important" (same source). The losses involve exploration, mazes and memory, the properties that matter in Super Mario Bros beyond 1-1. EDELINE (NeurIPS 2025) adds a Mamba memory over the whole trajectory to a DIAMOND-style model. At the same compute it lifts Atari 100k to a mean of 1.866 and a median of 0.817, and scores 4.1× DIAMOND on Crafter ([EDELINE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00466v2)). No official DIAMOND result exists for any NES game. The one community Mario port reports no metrics, as the next section shows.

The authors list three limitations: discrete control only, a four-frame memory they call "a minimal mechanism", and reward kept outside the diffusion model ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399)). The CS:GO model shows three failure modes. First, it "is more likely to drift out-of-distribution in less frequently visited areas of the map". Second, when visibility is lost it forgets the current state and generates "a new weapon or area of map". Third, it "wrongly enables successive jumps" because double jumps were rare in its data (same source). The most important line for your project is in the issue tracker. Asked about rare actions, the author wrote that "if the world model has not seen the effect of certain actions in certain states it may lead to modeling the effect of the modal action instead" ([issue #33](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond/issues/33)). Your whole exploration problem is actions whose effect is rare in particular states. The repository has had no author commits since December 2024. It has no general pipeline for training on your own game, and a November 2025 request for help with another game went unanswered ([repo](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond); [issue #44](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond/issues/44)).

## No downloadable model has learned the NES

DIAMOND publishes two kinds of checkpoint. There are **26 Atari 100k files, each holding a world model and a policy, about 54 MB each**, one seed per game. There is also **one 1.5 GB CS:GO world model** without a policy ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/eloialonso/diamond/tree/main)). They were trained on 64×64 Atari pixels and the 18-action Atari set, so none of them knows anything about NES graphics or the NES pad. The MIT-licensed code and the recipe are reusable; the weights are not. The loader also unpickles `.pt` files without `weights_only`, so a third-party fine-tune can run arbitrary code when you load it ([issue #30](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond/issues/30)). The Mario-specific models are small and weak. PlayGen is the strongest. Its predictions fall from a PSNR of 33.8 one frame ahead to 18.2 at 1,024 frames, and its action accuracy stays near 0.79–0.80 ([PlayGen](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00887v1)). But it simulates the Java "Mario AI Framework", whose graphics and physics differ from the NES ROM. MarioVGG knows only "run right" and "run right and jump", and press reports show Mario changing colour or turning into enemies ([Nintendo Life](https://nintendolife.com/news/2024/09/new-ai-model-can-recreate-super-mario-bros-footage-but-its-full-of-glitches)). The large open models were not built for a 2D game with an eight-button pad at 256×240. Their action encoders expect WASD keys and continuous mouse look, and their autoencoders are tuned for 3D or photorealistic scenes ([Matrix-Game 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.13009)). Adapting one would mean replacing the action module and fine-tuning a model of 0.5–5B parameters, a bigger job than training a small NES model from scratch.

In this field, "zero-shot" means turning a sketch or photo into a plausible scene of a genre the model was trained on. It does not mean simulating the rules of an unseen game. AVID tested this directly. A video model pretrained on 15 Procgen games, with no CoinRun action data, scored an **FVD of 204** on CoinRun (lower is better). Adapting it with **100k–2.5M action-labelled CoinRun samples** brought that to 56–14.5, at 3 days on one A100 per adaptation ([AVID](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12822v2)). Microsoft's WHAM card says images outside its game "dissolve into unrecognizable blobs" ([WHAM](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/wham)). GameBoyWorlds, a 2026 benchmark of 500 tasks in unseen Game Boy games, reports that world-modelling approaches fail ([GameBoyWorlds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32093)). Pretraining does not remove the need for the target game's own action-labelled frames. Public SMB data does exist. The best set has 737k frames across 280 episodes, with all 256 button combinations represented and a RAM snapshot for every frame ([smbdataset](https://github.com/rafaelcp/smbdataset)). Your emulator makes more data than that, from any state, whenever you ask.

Faithful game logic needs more data than a good first look suggests. GameNGen's ablation found that **1M examples render plausible views but get the game logic wrong**. Multiply by six games, or use a bigger model shared across them. You have one GPU, so every one of those GPU-days is a day without PPO training.

## DIAMOND dreams slower than your emulator plays

Your loop runs **13,900 decisions/s (55,600 frames/s)** on 12,288 consoles. Learning sets that limit, not the emulator. The policy encoder costs 0.9 GFLOP per decision, and the GPU runs at 24% of its peak FLOP rate (your measurements, 2026-11-15). DIAMOND's only first-party throughput figures come from its training profile on an RTX 4090 at batch 32. It takes **12.7 ms to predict a frame** (3 steps × 4.2 ms) and **20.4 ms per full imagined step**, including reward and action ([DIAMOND, App. I](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399); [config](https://github.com/eloialonso/diamond/blob/main/config/trainer.yaml)). That works out to about 2,500 predicted frames/s and 1,600 imagined transitions/s.

Dreaming at your rate would take about 41,700 U-Net passes per second (13,900 × 3), roughly 5.5× DIAMOND's published 7,600 at batch 32. A 5090 and bigger batches would close part of that gap, by an amount no one has published. My rough, unmeasured estimate is about 15 GFLOP per predicted 64×64 frame. That is about 17× your policy encoder's cost per decision. A learned simulator would therefore compete for the tensor-core time your learner already lacks, while the emulator uses almost none. The fastest open interactive model, Matrix-Game 4.0, makes about 900× fewer frames per second on the same GPU than your emulator does. Per real decision consumed, published world-model agents spend roughly 2,000× (STORM, 4.3 h per 100k steps) to 35,000× (DIAMOND) more wall-clock time than your loop ([STORM](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.09615); [DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399)). Your loop gets through the entire Atari 100k budget in about 7 seconds. Van Hasselt, Hessel and Aslanides made the general point. When a model only generates imagined transitions from states already observed, replaying those observed transitions does as well, and data-efficient Rainbow beat SimPLe "with less experience and computation" ([van Hasselt et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.05243)).

Speed is not the only mismatch. **Resolution:** NES frames have about 13× the pixels of 64×64. Shrinking 256×240 to 64×60 turns an 8×8 sprite into 2×2 pixels, which erases fireballs, Hammer Bros' hammers and Contra bullets, the very "visual details" in DIAMOND's title. Keeping full resolution means the CS:GO pipeline at 381M parameters. **RAM:** DIAMOND sees only pixels, while your policy reads the 2 KB RAM. A dreamed rollout would have to predict RAM as well, which nobody has tested, or train a policy that sees only pixels. **Memory:** four stacked frames at frameskip 4 cover about 16 emulator frames, a quarter of a second. Hidden blocks that have scrolled off screen, maze-castle loop state and star timers are invisible to the model. The authors expect scaling to fix most issues "with the exception of the memory of the model" ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399)). The emulator has none of these limits. It is exact, already batched, free of drift, and can save and restore any state.

## Sharper dreams have never bought better exploration

If a better next-screen model led to better exploration, DIAMOND would show it, because it draws Atari more faithfully than any earlier world model. It does not. On Private Eye, the one hard-exploration game in Atari 100k, DIAMOND scored **114.3 against a human 69,571**. That is about 0.13% of human level, essentially the same as IRIS at 100 despite much sharper frames ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399)). DreamerV3 scored 0 on the sparse-reward game Freeway at 100k steps (same source). The pattern holds at large budgets. MuZero scored **0 on Montezuma's Revenge and 0 on Pitfall after 20B frames** ([MuZero](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.08265)), and DreamerV3 scored 2,512 and 0 at 200M frames ([DreamerV3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04104v1)). DIAMOND explores only through random actions 1% of the time and an entropy weight of 0.001. One 2025 world-model agent, PoE-World, scores on Montezuma's Revenge in its comparison. It uses LLM-written symbolic rules over object lists plus a planner, and it collects only the first key, for 100 points. PPO at 20M steps scored 0 ([PoE-World](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10819)). No peer-reviewed world-model RL result exists for Super Mario Bros or any NES game.

The reason is now measured directly. Kang et al. trained video diffusion models on a 2D physics simulator. The models generalise "case-based": they copy the closest training example, matched by **colour first, then size, velocity and shape**, and "scaling alone is insufficient" to fix failures outside the training data ([Kang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02385)). A 2026 study of a 350M-parameter world model finds that hallucination concentrates where the data covers few state-action pairs, and calls it fundamentally a data-coverage problem ([Hansen & Wang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27326)). Three results already cited show the same effect: PlayGen's mushroom and fire-flower failures, DIAMOND's "modal action" warning, and its CS:GO drift in rarely visited areas. Models also invent abilities that do not exist. DIAMOND's CS:GO model allowed double jumps, and WorldCoder's learned model fired bullets in Montezuma's Revenge ([PoE-World](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10819)). A policy trained inside such a model can learn to exploit those errors ([WoVR](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13977)).

This splits your three entity questions cleanly. **What enemies do** is frequent and consistent. Mario stomps Goombas and dies on side contact thousands of times per hour of play, and a model would learn that. But your emulator already computes those outcomes exactly, and the RAM exposes them. **What power-ups do** is also learnable, but whether to take one is a question of value, not of prediction. In the most detailed Contra PPO baseline, a score reward drew the agent to the Laser, which is weak against the first boss, and the agent never beat that boss ([Contra baseline](https://kaihe.github.io/contra_agent/experiments/1_baseline.html)). A world model would not change that.

**Which pipe can be entered** is the question that defeats prediction. Enterable and decorative pipes look alike. A model trained on dozens of pipes where Down did nothing finds those cases and confidently predicts "nothing happens" at the 8-4 pipe. Every uncertainty-based bonus pays only for uncertainty: ensemble disagreement, BYOL-Explore, hallucination-as-curiosity. A confident wrong prediction therefore earns nothing. OPIQ was built around this effect. A network's generalisation wipes out optimism for untried state-action pairs unless counts restore it ([OPIQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12174)). Your own measurement on 2026-11-16 points the same way. From the stretch before the floating pipe, 0 of 8,192 random search-style button sequences and 0 of 256 policy attempts reached it within 40 decisions, and the policy pressed A there 0% of the time. That is a sampling problem, and a model of the screen cannot do the sampling for you.

One generic model across six games fares no better. The only evidence that one model can learn "what buttons do" across many games comes from Genie. Its latent actions kept consistent meanings across 2D platformers, after training on 30,000 hours of video with 10.7B parameters. It was never released and runs at about 1 FPS ([Genie](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15391)). Object-level inputs do help with entities. OC-STORM's object features raised its Hollow Knight win rate against the Mage Lord boss from 5% to 48% ([OC-STORM](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16443v1)), and your RAM is already a richer object input than a video segmenter. No world-model result exists for Contra, Adventure Island, Kage or Final Mission.

The strongest case for world models is real, but narrow. They help as a source of features: DreamerV3 "relies mostly on the unsupervised reconstruction loss rather than reward and value gradients" ([DreamerV3](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.04104v2)). They help as a curiosity signal: BYOL-Explore reported superhuman play on the ten hardest Atari exploration games ([BYOL-Explore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08332)), although noise that the agent's own presses trigger traps it immediately ([Jarrett et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10515)). They help as a guide to which transitions to replay: Curious Replay lifted DreamerV3 on Crafter from 14.5 to 19.4 ([Curious Replay](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15934v1)). None of these needs a pixel generator, and none finds an exception before it happens the first time.

## The useful pieces fit inside one PPO loop

The published evidence ranks the uses of a world model for a policy-gradient agent with plenty of data, like yours. Training in imagination and searching inside a learned model show measured gains only when samples are scarce. Hamrick et al. ablated MuZero and found that search helps mainly by building better learning targets and a better data distribution. Shallow rollouts did about as well as deep search, except on the hardest reasoning tasks ([Hamrick et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.04021)). The positive results at scale come from using the model as an extra training loss. Muesli is a policy-gradient agent that trains a MuZero-style model only as an extra loss and to compute one-step look-ahead targets. It reached a **median of 1,041% HNS on 57 Atari games at 200M frames**, level with MuZero Reanalyse at 1,047%, at "computation speed comparable to model-free baselines" ([Muesli](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.06159)). ATC, used as an extra loss on PPO, significantly improved 6 of 8 Atari games ([ATC](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.08319v3)).

| Use of a world model | Best published evidence | Cost in your loop | Finds the 8-4 pipe? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Train the policy in imagination | DIAMOND 1.459 HNS at 100k steps; replay matches it when data is cheap ([van Hasselt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.05243)) | slower than the emulator; takes GPU from learning | no |
| Tree search in a learned model | MuZero 0 on Montezuma and Pitfall at 20B frames ([MuZero](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.08265)) | inference per move × simulations | no |
| Extra model loss on the policy trunk | Muesli 1,041% median HNS at model-free speed; ATC + PPO better on 6 of 8 games | small head | no; better features only |
| Event and outcome heads | DFP won the 2016 ViZDoom Full Deathmatch ([review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.03470)) | tiny | could rank probes after pipes are seen elsewhere (untested) |
| Model error as curiosity or replay priority | BYOL-Explore on the 10 hardest Atari games; Curious Replay Crafter 14.5 → 19.4 | small | only after the first entry |
| Frozen pre-trained encoder or dynamics | AVID unadapted FVD 204 on a new game ([AVID](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12822v2)) | medium | no |
| Emulator branches (an exact world model) | interventional tests matched oracle return in 11 of 12 settings ([IBD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18257)) | ~5 min per full sweep | yes, with (place, button) counts |

The cheapest strong recipe predicts events, not screens. Direct Future Prediction (DFP) trained a network to predict future health, ammo and frags at several horizons for each possible action. It then chose actions by a weighted mix of those predictions, and won the Full Deathmatch track of the 2016 Visual Doom AI Competition ([DFP](https://ICLR.cc/archive/www/lib/exe/fetch.php%3Fmedia=iclr2017:dosovitskiy_koltun_learning_to_act_iclr_2017.pdf); [ViZDoom review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.03470)). For you, that means replacing the current `ram_logits` head. It predicts flips across all 16,384 RAM bits, and most of those bits are timers, random-number state and sound. The replacement is a small head on the GRU state plus a candidate button. It predicts a handful of events read from RAM at, say, 1, 4 and 16 decisions: death, change of power state, change of area (the pipe event), enemy killed, score change, and x-progress. Train it on injected and probe moves, not on the policy's own choices. Once the policy becomes nearly deterministic, labels from its own choices are biased.

Extra heads are sensitive to their loss weight. A ViZDoom study found performance "highly sensitive to the weighting placed on their losses" ([auxiliary tasks](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.00600)). PPO trunks can also lose representation quality over long runs ([Moalla et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00662v3)). Settle the weight with three matched seeds of each setup: no head; a head with gradients stopped at the trunk, which shows what the trunk already knows; and heads that train the trunk at weights 0.05, 0.2 and 0.5. In every run, log the ratio of the head's gradient norm to PPO's at the shared trunk.

Use the head's mistakes as a pointer, not as a reward. Where it predicts badly, probe more and replay more. That is the coverage-aware fix from Curious Replay and Hansen & Wang. The exceptions themselves come from the emulator. Restore one archived place into several consoles, hold a different button in each for a few decisions, and compare the RAM. Bytes that differ were caused by the press; bytes that agree changed on their own. Interventional tests of this kind matched oracle return in 11 of 12 settings with distractors, where methods that only observe often failed ([IBD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18257)). Choose which button to hold with a count over (place, button), so that Down gets tried on every pipe top once the other buttons have been tried there. OPIQ's optimism over untried state-action pairs explored 12 rooms of Montezuma's Revenge in 50M frames ([OPIQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12174)). Go-Explore's held random actions apply the same idea ([Go-Explore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)). A full sweep of 70,000 archived places × 8 buttons × 8 decisions is about 4.5M decisions, about five minutes of your loop. This is DIAMOND's "imagine what my action would do", done with a simulator that is exact, batched and already paid for.

One cheap experiment tests the hope directly, and nobody has run it yet. Train the event head on every SMB level except 8-4. Then, at the 8-4 floating pipe, ask whether it predicts an area change for Down before Down has ever been tried there. An above-chance answer means a learned prior that can rank probes across levels and games, the "knows what pipes do" knowledge you were reaching for. A chance-level answer means counts must carry the exceptions, as the literature predicts. On RAM targets the test runs in hours, not the days a pixel model would need. If you still want a pixel world model, as a demo or a curiosity source, build it outside the training loop. Start from the Super-Mario-DIAMOND recipe (64×60 plus an upsampler, about a day on one L4), add DIAMOND's CS:GO stability settings and PlayGen's balanced sampling of rare transitions, and add EDELINE's memory if long rollouts matter.

## Conclusion

Once the costs are counted, the question changes. Pre-training exists to save samples, and samples are the one thing you have in near-unlimited supply. What a world model would actually cost you is GPU time on one GPU that PPO already fills. A next-screen model would mostly add knowledge you least need: how common entities behave, which your emulator and RAM already compute exactly. The knowledge you lack is the one pipe, the one hidden block, the one boss-room move. Those are exactly what a model learned from data cannot predict before they have been tried. A pre-trained model does not change that. It only draws the wrong answer at higher resolution.

In plain words: do not download, fine-tune or train DIAMOND, or any other world model, to train your agent. Nothing pre-trained knows NES games. A model you trained yourself would be slower and less exact than your emulator, and it would still say "nothing happens" at the 8-4 pipe. Your emulator with save states is already the perfect world model, so do your imagining with it: branch a saved moment and try each button. Take one idea from DIAMOND, a small head that predicts what a button press will cause. Build it from a few RAM events instead of the whole screen, and test it with three seeds. Let count-driven button probes find the exceptions. Let PPO with self-imitation turn found routes into reliable play. Then run the 8-4 hold-out test, the one result that would show whether a learned prior can guide exploration across your six games.
