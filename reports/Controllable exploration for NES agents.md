# Probe each button; don't dream the game

Build the "what did my press cause" knowledge from interventions, not from watching. Your emulator can restore one saved state into several consoles, hold a different button in each, and compare the RAM. Bytes that differ between branches were caused by the press. Some effects are direct (Mario, the scroll, your bullets). Others are indirect (the enemy that died only in the fire branch, the area byte that changed only when Down was held on the pipe). Bytes that match in every branch moved on their own. The closest published method matched oracle return in 11 of 12 state-based tests, where methods that only observe often failed. The evidence does not support the hypothesis that a jointly learned world model, once it knows the buttons, completes all levels with light PPO. Learned models with tree search score **0 on Montezuma's Revenge and Pitfall after 20B frames**. The published hard-exploration wins spent more compute turning found routes into robust policies (10–50B frames) than finding them. Your 8-4 pipe is a sampling problem, not a modelling problem. Down does nothing on almost every pipe, so any learned model predicts "nothing happens" there, confidently, and pays no curiosity. Only counts over (place, button) or systematic button probes make the agent try it. Your curiosity bonus acted as screen novelty for the same reasons. Ensemble disagreement tracks unfamiliar screens, and a fingerprint of the whole frame is dominated by background. No published agent clears 8-4 or gets past Contra's first boss, and none plays S.C.A.T., Adventure Island, Kage or Batman. Your 4-4 result is already past the public evidence. The simplest recipe keeps PPO with a GRU and adds three cheap pieces inside the same loop. Counts over (place, button) choose the held random moves. A small share of consoles runs counterfactual button probes from archived places. A self-imitation term hands the policy the button that worked, not only the place.

## Interventions, not observation, separate your presses from the background

Pathak and colleagues split on-screen change into three kinds. The first is what the agent controls. The second is what it does not control but which can affect it. The third is what it neither controls nor is affected by. In their words, a good feature space "should model (1) and (2) and be unaffected by (3)" ([Pathak et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363)). Denoised MDPs sharpen the rule. They discard a factor only if it evolves independently of actions **and** does not affect the controllable state: "wind directions should not be ignored while sailing" ([Wang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15477)). In NES terms, Mario, the scroll and your own bullets are kind one. Enemies, enemy bullets and the level timer are kind two. Cloud drift and palette cycling are kind three. Kills, hits and blocks sit where kinds one and two meet. The enemy moves on its own, but whether it dies depends on your press. Any method you pick must keep that link.

Six families try to learn this split from data. The table shows what each keeps and how it breaks.

| Family | Examples | What it learns | Tested on | Main failure for NES |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One-step inverse dynamics | ICM, Burda's IDF, NGU, E3B, RIDE | features that predict the action from two consecutive frames | VizDoom with noise, MiniGrid, MiniHack, Mario 1-1 | drops enemies and bullets; merges distant look-alike states; misses effects that take several steps; keeps press-triggered junk |
| Contingency attention | CoEX | the screen cell that predicts the action (the player) | Montezuma's Revenge, Private Eye, Venture | assumes one controlled object; gives screen position, not world position |
| Multi-step inverse with a bottleneck | AC-State, PPE, ACRO | the first action of a k-step segment | robot arm, mazes, offline benchmarks | assumes the background never touches the agent; fails on periodic motion |
| Split world models | TIA, Denoised MDP, Iso-Dream, Iso-Dream++ | separate action-driven and action-free latent branches | DMC with video backgrounds, CARLA, a few Atari games | all information collapses into one branch; needs a reward signal; slower than Dreamer |
| Interventional tests | IBD, CAI | the per-variable effect of the action | state-based control, a Fetch robot | needs a factored state (NES RAM is one) |
| Latent actions from video | LAPO, Genie, LAOM | player-caused change without action labels | Procgen, platformer video | confused by distractors that move with the player |

One-step inverse models are the cheap default, and they do resist passive noise. ICM kept a perfect VizDoom score with **40% of the screen replaced by white noise**. The same agent predicting raw pixels "suffers significantly" ([Pathak et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363)). Their weaknesses are well mapped, and each one hits NES games. They keep only what helps predict the action, so they can miss "important aspects of the environment that the agent cannot immediately affect" ([Burda et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355)). In the game Catcher, inverse features failed to track the falling fruit, the closest analogue to an incoming bullet ([Rakelly et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07278)). They merge distant states that look alike locally. On a real robot, a one-step model merged three arm positions, "precisely what the theory predicts" ([Lamb et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08229)). They miss slow effects, failing when "it might take more than two consecutive steps" to see one ([Badia et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06038)). They get noisy labels when presses do nothing, such as mid-jump ([Choi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01483)). A kill shows up only if the hit lands inside the frame-skip window. They keep press-triggered junk. E3B agents farmed reward by pressing PAY or FIGHT, which made new messages but changed nothing in the game ([Henaff et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05805)). They learn biased labels when the policy is nearly deterministic. Early Freeway agents pressed mostly UP, so the action classifier was right "regardless of attention" ([Choi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01483)). And their features drift as training moves on, which makes any bonus built on them non-stationary ([Burda et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355)).

Contingency attention shows what a good self-locator is worth, and how far a learned one lags behind RAM. CoEX's inverse model must route its decision through a sparse attention map. The peak of that map becomes the player's position, on average 1–2 grid cells from the truth. It counted visits to that position, plus a room cluster and the score so far. That gave **11,618 on Montezuma's Revenge with PPO at 2B frames**. With the true position read from RAM instead, Private Eye rose from **5,316 to 24,296**, and Venture from 204 to 1,100 ([Choi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01483)). You already read position from RAM, so your agent already has this family's main idea.

Multi-step inverse models fix the merging of look-alike states. AC-State predicts the first action of a k-step segment through a discrete bottleneck. On a real robot it ignored a looping 4K video, drinking-bird toys, flashing lights and people. In a maze it ignored eight other agents moving at random ([Lamb et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08229)). Its guarantee rests on an assumption that NES games break: the background "has no interaction with the control-endogenous state" (same source). A Goomba that kills Mario interacts with him. ACDF also proved that multi-step inverse models fail on periodic dynamics "regardless of k", and that they need a forward-prediction loss as well ([Levine & Stone 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11940)). Fire bars, moving lifts and enemy walk cycles are all periodic.

Split world models try to keep both halves. Iso-Dream adds an "inverse cell" to an action-conditioned branch and keeps a separate action-free branch. It beat DreamerV2 on DMC with video backgrounds, for example 911 vs 655 on walker-walk ([Pan et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13817)). Its successor reported "training collapse": "most useful information may collapse into the action-conditioned branch". The fix requires the action branch's predictions to vary across candidate actions, and the action-free branch's predictions not to. Iso-Dream++ also models a "sparse dependency", such as a puck changing direction when hit ([Pan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14889)). It is the only published framework that matches kills and hits in concept. TIA, an earlier split model, needs informative rewards to pull its branches apart, and sparse early rewards do not supply them ([Fu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15612)). None of these was tested on NES games or crowded shooters.

The interventional family fits an emulator best. IBD treats the agent's actions as randomised interventions. It runs a two-sample test on each observation dimension and keeps the dimensions whose distributions shift. Across 12 state-based settings with up to 100 distractors, it **matched oracle return in 11**. The methods that only observe (mutual information, forward models, gradient sensitivity) "often underperform simply passing the full observation". They "can collapse when distractors mimic controllable state variables" ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18257)). CAI measures the mutual information between the action and each entity's next state. Given a factored state, it detected influence with an AUC of 0.97 on a robot pick-and-place task ([Seitzer et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03443)). Two more results say that true action labels matter. Learning from video without actions can be exponentially worse when the background noise is correlated over time ([Misra et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13765)). Adding true labels for just 2.5% of the data improved downstream performance about 4.2x on control tasks with distractors ([Nikulin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00379)).

Your emulator can run these interventions exactly, not just statistically. Restore one saved state into several consoles, hold a different button in each for a few decisions, and compare RAM. Bytes that end the same in every branch moved on their own: timers, frame counters, enemies that ignore Mario. Bytes that differ were caused by the press. Some effects are direct: Mario's position, the scroll, your bullets. Others are indirect: an enemy that died only in the fire branch, a homing enemy that turned, an area byte that changed only when Down was held on the pipe. The same diff works on frames, where the pixels that differ between branches are the ones your press touched. This handles kills, hits and defence by design, which no observation-only method guarantees. Its limits are just as clear. It sees only effects inside the probe horizon, and only for the buttons you probe. A maze-castle checkpoint that confirms the right path much later falls outside it. No paper has tested this on NES RAM. It is the IBD and CAI idea applied to a factored state you already have.

## Learned world models score zero where exploration is hard

A world model can replace PPO in two ways. Dreamer-style agents train the actor on imagined rollouts. They still use policy gradients, only on imagined data. MuZero-style agents plan with tree search inside a learned model and copy the search result into the policy. Only that family avoids policy gradients ([MuZero](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.08265); [DreamerV3](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.04104v2)). Both are strong on dense-reward Atari. EfficientZero V2 reaches a mean human-normalised score of 2.43 on Atari 100k ([EZ-V2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00564)).

They fail where your problem lives. **MuZero scored 0.00 on Montezuma's Revenge, 0.00 on Pitfall and 0.40 on Venture after 20B frames** ([MuZero, Table S1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.08265)). DreamerV3 reached 2,512 on Montezuma's Revenge and 0 on Pitfall and Venture at 200M frames ([DreamerV3 v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04104v1)). Its Minecraft diamonds needed a sequence of 12 milestone rewards ([DreamerV3 v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.04104v2)). BYOL-Explore is the world-model method with strong hard-exploration results, and it used the model as a curiosity signal, not as a planner ([BYOL-Explore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08332)). The reason is simple. A model predicts well only where the agent has been. Search inside it cannot plan towards an effect the data never showed.

That is the core problem with the hypothesis. "Knowing what each button does" mixes two kinds of knowledge. The first is general: A jumps, B fires, Right scrolls, Down crouches. A model learns this quickly, and it transfers between levels. The second is a local exception: Down on this one pipe in 8-4 enters it. A model learns an exception only after the agent has tried it there. Before that first try, a model trained on hundreds of pipes where Down did nothing predicts nothing here, and does so confidently. OPIQ was built around this effect. It keeps count-based optimism in both action choice and bootstrapping, because a network's generalisation otherwise wipes out the optimism for untried state-action pairs ([Rashid et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12174)). The model inherits the exploration problem. It does not solve it.

Large-scale attempts show the same gap between knowing the controls and making progress. VPT trained an inverse dynamics model, pseudo-labelled about 70,000 hours of Minecraft video, and cloned the result. Even after RL fine-tuning, it got the diamond pickaxe only about 2.5% of the time ([VPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11795); [JARVIS-1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05997)). It used 720 GPUs for 9 days ([DreamerV3 v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04104v1)). Dreamer 4 was trained offline on 2,541 hours of human play and got diamonds in 0.7% of episodes ([Dreamer 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.24527v1)). A community JEPA world model trained on Super Mario Bros. led its author to conclude that predicting the game is not the same as learning to make progress ([LeMario](https://www.promptzone.com/anika_bhat/lemario-trains-jepa-world-model-on-super-mario-bros-1d42.md)). That one is an unreviewed post.

Three practical costs add to this. First, pixel world models drop small sprites. L2 reconstruction is "dominated by large areas" ([OC-STORM](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16443v1)). IRIS imagined enemies as rewards in Asterix. DIAMOND's diffusion model fixed that, at about 2.9 days on an RTX 4090 per 100k-step run ([DIAMOND](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399)). NES bullets are 8×8 sprites on a scrolling screen. Second, throughput. DreamerV3 ran Atari at about 36 agent steps per second on a V100 (50M steps in 16 GPU-days) ([DreamerV3 v1, Table A.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04104v1)). Your emulator makes 14,000 decisions per second, about 1.2B a day. A Dreamer-style learner at published replay ratios would leave most of that idle. Tree search multiplies inference cost by the number of simulations, which is 16 per move for EZ-V2 on Atari ([EZ-V2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00564)). Third, you already own a perfect action-conditioned world model. The emulator predicts every next frame exactly, and it can save and restore. No learned model will beat it as a simulator.

The published evidence also says the expensive step is not finding a route. It is turning the route into a reliable policy. Go-Explore spent 2B frames exploring Montezuma's Revenge and then **10B frames of PPO robustifying** the result, for 43,791 points ([Ecoffet et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)). The backward curriculum from one human demo took about 50B frames on 128 GPUs. It needed "careful tuning of the coefficient of the entropy bonus". Its 74,500-point policy fell to about 10,000 under sticky actions ([Salimans & Chen 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)). That is the opposite of light PPO fine-tuning.

The hypothesis does hold in a weaker form: low-level skills transfer and speed up new levels. In Mario, a curiosity policy from Level-1 fine-tuned on Level-2 for 1.5M steps did slightly better than 3.5M steps from scratch (466 vs 455.5 distance). Fine-tuning on Level-3, though, did worse than running the policy unchanged (97.5 vs 319.3) ([Pathak et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363)). Inverse-dynamics features survived the day-to-night change from 1-1 to 1-3, where random features did not ([Burda et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355)). Joint PPO across Sonic training levels doubled plain PPO's test score (3,128 vs 1,489). But zero-shot performance "does not increase much after the first few million timesteps" ([Nichol et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.03720)). The one clear "prior plus light PPO" success is LAPO. Its latent policy recovered expert Procgen performance with 4M PPO frames, where PPO from scratch reached 44% of expert. But its prior came from 8M frames of an expert playing, not from button knowledge ([Schmidt & Jiang 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10812)). DreamerV3 itself "relies mostly on the unsupervised reconstruction loss rather than reward and value gradients" ([DreamerV3 v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.04104v2)), which supports world models as representation learners. The verdict: keep a jointly trained action model as an auxiliary head and an exploration aid. Do not expect it to remove PPO, or to find rare presses.

## Only counts and archives pay for a press nobody has tried

Most exploration bonuses reward an outcome. RND, ICM, NGU, E3B, RIDE, NovelD and BYOL-Explore all pay when something new appears. None of them pays for trying Down on a familiar pipe until the pipe has already been entered. ICM's Mario agent stopped at a pit that needed "a very specific sequence of 15-20 key presses" ([Pathak et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363)). Your 8-4 pipe is the same class of problem.

Three published mechanisms push untried actions in familiar states directly. Go-Explore returns to an archived cell, then takes random actions for 100 steps. Each action repeats the previous one with 95% probability, so a press is held about 20 steps on average ([Ecoffet et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)). OPIQ adds optimistic state-action count bonuses both when choosing actions and when bootstrapping, so untried pairs stay attractive. It reliably explored 12 rooms of Montezuma's Revenge in 50M frames ([Rashid et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12174)). CAI samples actions and picks the one whose predicted outcome deviates most from the average. That was about 2x more sample-efficient than random exploration on robot tasks ([Seitzer et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03443)). Only the first two can find a true exception. CAI needs the model to already suspect that the action matters.

The literature explains why your curiosity bonus fell into the outcome trap. Ensemble disagreement measures the model's ignorance. It drops to near zero on pure noise and stays high on unfamiliar inputs ([Pathak et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04161)). On a new screen, the heads disagree for every button, so the bonus tracks the screen. The target makes this worse. A fingerprint of the whole next frame is dominated by what covers most pixels: scroll, enemies, background ([OC-STORM](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16443v1)). Mario's own change is a small patch. Many press effects, such as a jump arc, a bullet or a pipe animation, appear after the next frame, which one-step targets miss ([Badia et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06038)). And the heads generalise across places, so a pipe where Down has never been tried looks like every pipe where it did nothing ([Rashid et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12174)). Two changes would make the bonus action-specific, though neither has been tested at scale. First, predict controllable RAM bytes instead of a frame fingerprint. Second, pay for the contrast: how much the heads disagree about the chosen button's outcome relative to the average over all buttons. That is CAI's quantity, and it echoes Iso-Dream++'s rule that predictions should vary across actions ([Pan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14889)). Even fixed, it would not find a true exception. Counts would.

The noisy-TV problem splits into passive noise and press-triggered noise. That split decides which bonuses survive.

| Bonus | Passive background motion | Press-triggered noise | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pixel prediction error | fails | fails | "performs bad across all the environments" ([Burda et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355)) |
| ICM (forward error on inverse features) | survives a 40% white-noise patch | drawn to a ball that changes colour on a press | ([Pathak et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363)); ([Raileanu & Rocktäschel 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12292)) |
| RIDE (impact with episodic count) | not reported | survives the colour-changing ball | ([Raileanu & Rocktäschel 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12292)) |
| RND | deterministic target avoids stochastic-transition traps | "suffers greatly" with on-demand pixel noise | ([Burda et al. RND](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12894)); ([Jarrett et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10515)) |
| BYOL-Explore | not reported | "trapped immediately"; flatlines on sticky-action Montezuma | ([Jarrett et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10515)) |
| BYOL-Hindsight | not reported | matches its noise-free performance | ([Jarrett et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10515)) |
| Ensemble disagreement | near-zero reward on pure noise | beats baseline with an action-switched TV | ([Pathak et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04161)) |
| Learning progress (LPM) | reward reaches zero on Noisy MNIST in about 150 steps | small drops on authors' own Atari action-noise variants | ([Hou et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25438)) |
| Counts on raw observations | fail when a timer makes every state unique | not reported | ([Henaff et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05805)) |
| Counts on RAM position | immune: the key ignores the screen | immune unless the press moves the key | inference, untested |

NES games mix both kinds. Enemies, scroll and timers are passive. Pause, sound-linked animation and HUD changes are triggered by presses. A count keyed on RAM position ignores both, because the key changes only when the player moves. A count over the full RAM does not. Timers and random-number bytes make every state unique, which is the trap E3B describes ([Henaff et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05805)).

Episodic memory matters as well. RIDE, AGAC and NovelD "fail to learn" without their episodic count term ([Henaff et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05805)). But global bonuses beat episodic ones when the layout repeats across episodes: 0.96 vs 0.10 on a fixed Corridors task. Multiplying the two is robust. Adding them is not, because the global term decays and the episodic term takes over ([Henaff et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03236)). NES levels are fixed layouts, the case where global novelty matters. If your per-game payment is already scaled by a cross-game rarity term, it is the multiplicative kind. Keep it that way when you add terms.

So the bonus that matches "an untried press in a familiar place" is a count over (place, button), keyed on the RAM position you already use. It resists the noisy TV because the key ignores the screen. Its main risk is farming, the E3B message-spam pattern. If every untried button in every 8×8 block pays, the agent can wander around pressing things. Use the count mainly to choose the held exploratory moves. Keep any reward from it small and decaying.

## No published agent clears 8-4 or gets past Contra's first boss

Evidence on Super Mario Bros. is thin and mostly community-made. The best documented result trains one PPO model per level and completes 31 of 32 levels. 4-4 and 7-4 were solved under an unexplained "hardcore setting", and 8-4 "has not been solved yet". Level 1-3 needed a learning rate of 7e-5 after about 70 failed attempts ([uvipen](https://github.com/uvipen/Super-mario-bros-PPO-pytorch)). One repo claims a single model for all 32 stages. It reports no success rates, and it turns 7-4's "puzzle solved" sound into reward ([dgriff777](https://github.com/dgriff777/SuperMarioRL)). Curiosity alone crossed about 30% of 1-1 with A3C ([Pathak et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363)). With 2,048 parallel environments it discovered 11 levels, secret rooms and boss fights, but never finished the game ([Burda et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355)). No peer-reviewed world-model agent result was found for Super Mario Bros. or any NES game. Your agent clears 4-4 at 72–95% from its own exploration. That is already beyond anything documented for the maze castles. 8-4 is open ground.

Run-and-gun games are further behind. The most detailed Contra baseline ran PPO for 32M steps on 32 environments. It navigated, jumped and shot well, but it "consistently fails to defeat the Level 1 boss". The diagnosed causes were simple. Fire and move were mutually exclusive actions. A score reward made it pick up the Laser weapon, which is weak against the boss. And the delay from pickup to death was hundreds of steps ([kaihe Contra baseline](https://kaihe.github.io/contra_agent/experiments/1_baseline.html)). A Mega Man agent used a distance map that penalised moving backwards more than it rewarded moving forwards. It pre-trained for more than 25M steps on a ROM with the enemies removed, and it trained the boss fight separately. It has no full run ([megai_man](https://github.com/victorsevero/megai_man)). A Touhou bullet-hell PPO agent reached stage 2 after about 20 days ([thrl](https://github.com/touhourl/thrl)). These sources contain no RL results for S.C.A.T./Final Mission, Adventure Island, Kage or Batman.

Hard-exploration Atari is where these methods were actually compared.

Four patterns stand out. Memory separates the winners on Pitfall. RND scores −3, while Go-Explore, NGU, Agent57 and BYOL-Explore score in the thousands. Pitfall's rooms look alike, much like looping maze castles. The top results also restore emulator states or follow a demonstration. On Sonic, JERK, a scripted search that replays its best action sequence, beat plain PPO, "likely due to JERK's perfect memory" ([Nichol et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.03720)). The budgets fit your machine. At 14,000 decisions per second you make about 1.2B decisions a day. If a decision is 4 frames, that is about 4.8B frames a day. The 2–12B-frame budgets above are half a day to three days of your compute, though some of them used bigger networks. Finally, stacking bonuses can hurt. In Craftax, with 4,096 parallel environments running 1B steps in about 51 minutes, "none of the tested exploration methods" (RND, ICM, E3B) improved PPO ([Craftax](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16801)). In Mario, adding ICM to the game reward "hurts learning" ([Raileanu & Rocktäschel 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12292)).

The evidence on generalisation is consistent and sobering. Procgen agents needed "as many as 10,000 levels" to close the gap between training levels and unseen ones. A fixed order of levels gave an "illusion of meaningful progress" ([Procgen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01588)). On Sonic's test levels, joint PPO scored 3,128 against 7,438 for humans. The best contest entry reached 4,692 of a possible 10,000 on the contest's own test set, and one top team added training levels from other Sonic games ([Nichol et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.03720); [OpenAI Retro Contest](https://openai.com/index/retro-contest-results/)). Six games with fixed levels sit far below 10,000 levels. Expect shared skills that make each new level faster to learn, not zero-shot completion of an unseen game.

## One GPU, one loop: archive, count, probe, imitate, robustify

Keep what already works: PPO with a GRU, RAM and screen inputs, RAM-keyed position novelty, and restarts from archived places. Add three cheap pieces inside the same loop. Skip world-model imagination and tree search. Every piece below trains or collects data in the one training loop, so nothing is trained separately.

| Piece | What it does | Evidence | Cost on your setup |
|---|---|---|---|

Start with the exploration moves. Today the held random moves are occasional and uniform. Make them count-driven. When the loop injects a held move, pick the button tried least often at the current 8×8 block. Hold it for several decisions, as Go-Explore's 95% repeat does. On the 8-4 floating pipe, Down becomes the obvious pick once the other buttons have been tried there. This does not depend on the policy's own chance of pressing Down. PPO pushes that chance towards zero, because Down almost never pays anywhere else.

Then give a small share of consoles to probes. Each probe restores an archived place and copies it into several branches. Each branch holds a different button, or a short combo, for k decisions. Then the probe compares RAM across the branches. The arithmetic shows how cheap this is. Suppose the archive holds 70,000 places, and each probe tries 8 buttons for 8 decisions. A full sweep then costs about 4.5M decisions, about 5 minutes at 14,000 decisions per second. Any branch that reaches a new area goes straight into the archive. The diffs also yield, per game, a mask of which RAM bytes respond to presses. That could build count keys for new games without hand-picked addresses, but this use is untested. Whether one saved state per 8×8 block lands inside the 10-pixel pipe window depends on what your archive keeps there. Check it directly.

The effect head is the jointly learned model of what the buttons do. Train a small head on the GRU state to predict, for each button, which controllable bytes it will change relative to the other buttons. Train it only on probe labels and on injected moves, never on the policy's own choices. Those choices bias the labels, as Freeway showed ([Choi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01483)). Use the predicted effect size to rank which places and buttons to probe first. After it has seen Down enter pipes in earlier levels, it should rate Down on a pipe top as worth probing in 8-4. That is the human-like prior your hypothesis wants. The transfer is plausible, not proven.

Next, hand each discovery to the policy. Restarting from a place tells the policy where to be, not which button worked there. When a probe or an injected move produces a new place, store the (state, button) pair. Add a self-imitation loss on those pairs, as policy-based Go-Explore did ([Ecoffet et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)). Also restart a few decisions before the discovery. The backward curriculum showed that this step is where the compute goes and where entropy tuning matters ([Salimans & Chen 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)).

Keep a next-RAM prediction head as an auxiliary loss. ACDF shows that inverse-only representations need a forward loss ([Levine & Stone 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11940)), and DreamerV3 relies mainly on its reconstruction loss. A multi-step inverse head is cheap to add. It predicts an injected button from GRU states k decisions apart. AC-State's method assumes that the labelled action was random ([Lamb et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08229)). Run an ablation on both heads, because neither has been shown to help PPO on NES games. For generalisation, train all levels of all games in one run, with a random start place for each episode. That is the closest you can get to Procgen's many-level regime. Use the same 8-button pad across games, and evaluate under sticky actions.

Some published options are not worth their cost here. E3B's per-console covariance matrix would take about 3 GB at 256 dimensions and 12,288 consoles, by simple arithmetic. It also ran 1.5–2x slower in wall-clock time in its own paper ([Henaff et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05805)). Pixel prediction-error bonuses, Dreamer imagination and tree search fail for the reasons above.

### Pitfalls that have already bitten similar agents

Farming comes first. Any fine-grained count can be farmed, and the agent will find the cheapest presses that move the key. Normalise each intrinsic stream by a running estimate of its return's standard deviation. Use separate extrinsic and intrinsic value heads; RND used discounts of 0.999 and 0.99 ([Burda et al. RND](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12894)). Agent57 found that separate value networks "significantly increase the training stability" ([Badia et al. Agent57](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350)).

Entropy cuts both ways. "If the agent's actions are too random, it makes too many mistakes … if the actions are too deterministic, the agent stops learning" ([Salimans & Chen 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)). Count-driven injected moves let you keep policy entropy lower without losing exploration. Low entropy also corrupts inverse-model labels, which is why those heads should train only on injected moves.

Determinism breeds memorisation. A 74,500-point policy dropped to about 10,000 under sticky actions (same source). A probe that works once in a noise-free emulator can be a fluke, so robustify a route under noise before you trust it. Go-Explore names the opposite failure "derailment": exploratory noise stops the agent from returning to the states it wants to explore from ([Ecoffet et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)).

Deaths and resets need care. Burda's curiosity agents had the "done" signal removed. They still avoided death, because the restart was predictable and therefore boring ([Burda et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355)). A bonus that resets on every life could instead reward dying, because the cheap start looks new again. That is an extrapolation, not a measured result. Episodes that span the whole game avoid it.

Learned features drift. CoEX's position error spiked whenever a new scene appeared ([Choi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01483)), so never key counts on a learned embedding when a RAM key exists. In shooters, score rewards pull agents towards bad weapon choices, and boss rooms give zero horizontal progress. Both need their own rewards or save states ([kaihe Contra baseline](https://kaihe.github.io/contra_agent/experiments/1_baseline.html)). In maze castles, horizontal progress is deceptive. The only 7-4 fix on record uses a level-specific sound, which will not generalise ([dgriff777](https://github.com/dgriff777/SuperMarioRL)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. You do not need to learn a world model, because you already have an exact one with save states. The open problems are where to spend probes, and how to hand a discovery to the policy so that it survives noise. The hypothesis treats "knows what each button does" as one thing. It is two. General button knowledge is learnable, transfers and speeds things up. Local exceptions, like Down on one pipe among dozens, can only be found by trying them. They must be counted rather than modelled, because a model's generalisation erases exactly the optimism that would point at them. The same split shows why kills and hits need interventions. Watching cannot tell a Goomba that died from your fireball apart from one that walked off a ledge. Comparing a branch where you fired with one where you did not can.

Several things here are untested anywhere. Nobody has published counterfactual RAM probes, (place, button) counts or effect heads on NES games. Nobody has tested kills or reactive enemies with any controllability method on Atari or NES. The cheapest decisive experiment runs on 8-4 alone. Swap uniform held moves for count-driven ones. Measure how often Down is tried on the floating pipe and how soon the pipe is first entered. Then check whether an effect head trained on the other levels ranks Down on that pipe top above chance before it has ever been tried there. If both work, you have the mechanism your hypothesis was reaching for. Learned knowledge of what presses do drives exploration, and counts cover the cases that knowledge cannot predict. PPO still does the heavy lifting of making it reliable.
